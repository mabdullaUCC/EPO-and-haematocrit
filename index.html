Normal (rest)

  • Haematocrit: ~40–45%
  • Basal EPO production
  • Oxygen delivery supports erythropoiesis

Well-trained athlete

  • Illustrative training-associated phenotype: haematocrit ~48–50%
  • EPO may be slightly elevated during training-related stimulation
  • Red-cell mass may increase; plasma-volume expansion often keeps haematocrit normal or lower in endurance athletes

Aplastic anaemia

  • Markedly reduced haematocrit
  • EPO ↑↑ due to reduced oxygen delivery
  • Marrow cannot respond adequately; reticulocytes low

High altitude

  • Lower inspired PO₂ stimulates EPO
  • Haematocrit increases over weeks
  • Tissue oxygenation may remain reduced despite adaptation
  • Acute: renal hypoxia → EPO ↑↑ before red-cell mass increases
  • Weeks: haematocrit ↑ while EPO falls from its peak (may approach baseline)

Blood doping (sport)

  • Transfusion or exogenous EPO can increase RBC mass and haematocrit
  • Endogenous EPO often suppressed after RBC mass rises
  • Increased viscosity and thrombosis risk; prohibited in sport

Polycythaemia vera

  • Haematocrit ↑↑ due to autonomous marrow production
  • EPO usually ↓ (negative feedback)
  • Hyperviscosity can impair microvascular flow

Physiology teaching note: Curves and condition markers are conceptual, not measured clinical reference data. The visual crossing at 40% is by design, not an equality of EPO and tissue PO₂ (different units). The well-trained athlete marker at 50% represents a selected training-associated phenotype with mildly elevated EPO; resting EPO is often normal and haematocrit can be normal or reduced because of plasma-volume expansion. At high altitude, the two special markers show low renal oxygenation and high EPO during acute exposure; the generic red curve does not apply without accounting for inspired oxygen. High altitude and exogenous EPO use alter the relationship between haematocrit, renal oxygen sensing and circulating EPO; therefore condition markers should not be interpreted as exact values on the generic curves. For blood doping, endogenous EPO may be suppressed after transfusion, while an assay may detect administered EPO after exogenous EPO use. Increasing haematocrit does not guarantee increased tissue oxygenation, particularly at very high values.
